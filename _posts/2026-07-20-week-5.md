Week 5: Depression Screening, Housing Stability, and Reproducible SDOH Workflows

During Week 5, I continued building on the 30-day hospital readmission workflow by expanding the analysis from alcohol screening into additional Social Determinants of Health domains. This week’s work focused mainly on Depression screening data, while also beginning to investigate Housing Stability as another important SDOH domain. This aligns with my broader project goal of cleaning, merging, and analyzing encounter and SDOH data to study alcohol use, depression, housing stability, prior healthcare utilization, and SDOH nonresponse in relation to 30-day readmission.

A major part of this week involved cleaning and standardizing the Depression SDOH data. I worked through the raw response values and organized the screening information into more consistent categories that could be analyzed alongside the hospital encounter dataset. This included combining PHQ-2 questions and classifying PHQ-2, Edinburgh, and self-harm responses as either positive or negative where appropriate.

I also restored important response-quality flags, including declined responses, unable-to-answer responses, missing responses, and conflicting responses. This was an important methodological step because unclear or incomplete responses should not automatically be discarded. In earlier analyses, I saw that nonresponse patterns may still carry useful information, so I wanted to preserve these categories instead of losing them during cleaning.

After cleaning the Depression screening data, I merged it with the hospital encounter dataset. I retained the prior-utilization features and the same 30-day follow-up requirements used in the earlier Alcohol analysis. This helped keep the Depression workflow consistent with the previous analysis and made the results easier to compare across SDOH domains.

I then analyzed associations between Depression screening results and 30-day readmission. One of the most notable findings was that positive self-harm responses showed a statistically significant association with 30-day readmission, with a p-value of .002. Other Depression screening comparisons were not statistically significant. I treated these results cautiously because statistical association does not prove causation, and screening variables may reflect broader clinical, behavioral, or social risk factors.

I also labeled the combined overall Depression measure as exploratory because it is not a validated standalone screening instrument. Although combining multiple Depression-related indicators may be useful for early analysis, it should not be presented as equivalent to a clinically validated depression scale. This was an important reminder that feature engineering decisions need to be both computationally useful and clinically defensible.

In addition to the Depression analysis, I began investigating Housing Stability as another SDOH domain. This is important because housing-related instability may influence hospital use, follow-up care, access to resources, and readmission risk. My goal is to continue evaluating whether SDOH domains such as housing, depression, alcohol use, and other screening areas contribute meaningful information to the readmission prediction workflow.

A major accomplishment this week was standardizing the Depression workflow and file structure so that it aligns with the earlier Alcohol analysis. I created a reproducible notebook, reusable scripts, statistical CSV outputs, an Excel workbook, and a PDF research summary. This structure will make it easier to repeat the same type of analysis for additional SDOH domains and compare results across domains.

Overall, Week 5 helped me move from analyzing one SDOH area in isolation toward building a more reproducible, domain-by-domain research pipeline. I am learning how to clean raw healthcare screening data, preserve meaningful response categories, merge SDOH data with encounter-level outcomes, apply statistical testing, and document results in a way that can support future machine learning modeling. This work also prepares the project for later decision-tree and Random Forest modeling, where cleaned and carefully defined SDOH features may be evaluated for prediction, feature importance, and interpretability.
