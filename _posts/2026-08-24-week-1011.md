Weeks 10 and 11: Writing the Final Report, Revising My Presentation, and Responding to Research Feedback
During Weeks 10 and 11, I focused on turning the technical work from my DREAM project into a complete research paper and a clearer final presentation. After spending much of the summer building the 30-day hospital readmission pipeline, I shifted my attention toward explaining the project’s methods, results, limitations, and clinical relevance in a form that other researchers could review and understand.
A major part of these two weeks involved writing and revising my final technical report. I organized the paper into sections covering the research background, related literature, cohort construction, feature engineering, machine learning methods, model evaluation, diagnosis-transition analysis, limitations, and conclusions. Writing the report required me to connect many separate stages of the project into one consistent research narrative.
I paid particular attention to describing the project’s leakage controls and patient-level validation strategy. The final analysis used 97,310 eligible hospital encounters from 59,449 patients. Patients were separated across the training, validation, and held-out test partitions so that the same patient could not appear in more than one partition. I also documented how model settings and clinical operating thresholds were selected using development data before the test set was evaluated.
Another important addition to the paper was the feature-importance analysis. I compared permutation importance across logistic regression, histogram gradient boosting, Random Forest, and XGBoost. Prior high acute-care utilization during the preceding 365 days was the strongest consensus feature, followed by pregnancy-related diagnosis context. Several SDOH documentation variables, including missing or unavailable screening records, also appeared among the leading features.
These findings required careful interpretation. A feature’s importance does not mean that it causes readmission, and documentation-related variables may reflect screening practices, healthcare access, clinical workflow, or patient complexity. I therefore worked on explaining the difference between predictive importance, statistical significance, and causation. This helped make the report more scientifically cautious and clinically responsible.
I also continued revising the final presentation after receiving good feedback from Dr. Tchoua. Her feedback helped me improve how I communicated the project and decide which technical details needed more explanation. I revised the slides to make the research question, cohort, predictor groups, model comparisons, feature importance, threshold results, and diagnosis-transition analysis easier to follow.
The presentation revisions also included improvements to the organization and visual consistency of the deck. I clarified table labels, strengthened explanations of the modeling workflow, improved the readability of the results and references, and made the main findings more prominent. I also added context explaining that the strongest model achieved moderate discrimination and should be considered a research tool for possible risk stratification rather than a system ready for autonomous clinical decisions.
Receiving positive feedback from Dr. Tchoua was encouraging because it showed that the project had developed substantially from the early exploratory work. At the beginning of the summer, I was learning how to inspect large healthcare datasets, connect encounter and SDOH records, and prepare categorical variables. By Weeks 10 and 11, I was writing a complete technical report and presenting a reproducible comparison of 28 machine learning models across seven feature sets.
These weeks also reminded me that research communication is an iterative process. Completing an analysis does not automatically make the findings understandable. The writing and presentation must clearly describe what was done, why each decision was made, what the results mean, and what the results cannot establish. Feedback from an advisor is especially valuable because it helps identify areas that may be clear to the researcher but unclear to an outside audience.
Overall, Weeks 10 and 11 were devoted to transforming the project into a more complete research contribution. Writing the paper helped me connect the literature, methods, results, and limitations. Revising the presentation helped me communicate the same work more clearly and efficiently. Dr. Tchoua’s positive feedback and recommendations helped strengthen both deliverables and gave me greater confidence in explaining the technical and clinical significance of my work.
These activities marked an important stage in my development as a computer science researcher. I gained experience not only in healthcare data analysis and machine learning, but also in academic writing, interpreting feature importance, responding to feedback, and presenting research responsibly.
