Week 7: Matched-Domain Analyses, Validation Planning, and Machine Learning Preparation

During Week 7, I focused on standardizing the SDOH analysis workflow and connecting my current work more directly to the future machine learning phase of the project. After expanding the analysis to additional SDOH domains, I worked on creating matched-domain analyses with consistent research questions, standardized comparison groups, patient-clustered statistical models, and clear reporting of which comparisons could or could not be estimated.

This week helped me understand the importance of making each domain analysis comparable. For each SDOH domain, I aimed to ask similar questions: whether positive responses, negative responses, nonresponse categories, or missing/not-screened responses were associated with readmission, combined ED-or-hospital return, or prior utilization. Using a consistent structure makes it easier to compare findings across domains and helps prepare the data for a future modeling matrix.

I also investigated several limitations that could affect interpretation. These included low SDOH screening coverage, nonrandom missingness, repeated patients, limited response variation, and the possibility of incorrectly linking an SDOH response from a different encounter. These limitations are important because healthcare data are not always collected evenly or consistently. A missing response may reflect documentation workflow, patient access, clinical acuity, or screening practices rather than the true absence of a social need.

Another important part of Week 7 was reviewing the history of Dr. Tchoua’s related research. I looked more closely at how earlier studies handled feature sets, correlation filtering, Random Forest importance, repeated experiments, and leakage. This helped me understand how my current work connects to prior SCHOLAR-related research while also identifying opportunities to strengthen validation and reproducibility.

This review also helped me think more carefully about data leakage. In a readmission prediction project, it is important that the model only uses information that would realistically be available before the outcome occurs. I began refining the modeling plan to prevent temporal leakage, split data at the patient level, and avoid allowing information from the same patient to appear in both training and testing sets. These decisions are important for producing a more trustworthy estimate of model performance.

I also refined the future modeling plan around feature blocks. One goal is to compare baseline models, EHR-based models, and models that add SDOH features. This would help test whether SDOH data provide additional predictive value beyond encounter or clinical information alone. I also plan to retain meaningful nonresponse categories rather than automatically dropping or combining them, since earlier analyses suggested that declined, unable, unknown, and missing/not-screened responses may carry different information.

The modeling direction will include decision-tree and Random Forest methods. Random Forest models are especially relevant because they can support prediction while also providing feature-importance information. This may help identify which variables contribute most to readmission prediction and whether SDOH features add meaningful interpretive value. At the same time, I am learning that feature importance must be interpreted carefully, especially when variables are correlated or when missingness patterns influence the model.

Overall, Week 7 helped me move from statistical domain analyses toward a clearer machine learning strategy. I am now thinking more intentionally about patient-level splitting, leakage prevention, feature-block comparison, nonresponse preservation, and whether SDOH features improve predictive performance and interpretability. This week strengthened the connection between the exploratory statistical work and the future modeling phase of my DREAM project.
