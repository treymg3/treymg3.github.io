Week 8: Building the Complete Readmission Pipeline and Evaluating Machine Learning Models

During Week 8, I brought the major parts of my summer research together into a complete and reproducible 30-day hospital readmission pipeline. My work progressed from analyzing individual Social Determinants of Health domains to building standardized modeling matrices, comparing machine learning approaches, and preparing the project’s final documentation and presentation materials.
I first established a fixed analytic cohort of 97,310 eligible hospital encounters with complete 30-day follow-up. This cohort included 11,973 encounters followed by a hospital readmission within 30 days. I preserved the same encounters and outcome definition throughout the modeling process so differences in model performance would reflect the predictors being added rather than changes in the study population.
I then created three nested modeling matrices. The baseline matrix, M0, included prior acute-care utilization during the preceding 365 days. M1 added clinically meaningful primary-diagnosis groups derived from ICD-10-CM codes and the AHRQ Clinical Classifications Software Refined system. M2 added reduced, mutually exclusive features representing 12 SDOH domains, including alcohol use, depression, financial strain, food insecurity, housing instability, intimate partner violence, physical activity, smoking, social connection, stress, transportation, and utilities-related hardship.
An important part of this process was preserving the meaning of incomplete documentation. Responses such as unable to answer, declined, unresolved, and no record were not automatically treated as negative responses. I also developed reproducible rules for handling sparse categories, duplicate variables, unresolved diagnoses, and conflicting SDOH responses.
To reduce the risk of data leakage, I created patient-separated development and test sets with no patient appearing in both. All feature decisions, preprocessing, model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and threshold selection were completed without using the locked test set. I also reviewed the predictor lists to exclude identifiers, future-event information, outcome proxies, and other fields that would not be available when a prediction is made.
I evaluated seven feature-block combinations across logistic regression and tree-based methods. I later expanded the unified pipeline to four classifier families and 28 comparable model experiments. The strongest held-out result came from the full baseline, EHR, and SDOH feature set using XGBoost, which achieved an AUROC of approximately 0.666. The full feature set also performed best, or effectively best, across logistic regression, Random Forest, and histogram gradient boosting. These results suggest that EHR information contributed most of the improvement in discrimination, while SDOH variables provided smaller incremental information when combined with the baseline and EHR features. However, the overall performance remains moderate and is not sufficient for autonomous clinical use.
I also completed a descriptive analysis of the diagnoses recorded during readmission. Among readmitted encounters, the diagnosis frequently crossed clinical body systems rather than representing an exact recurrence of the index diagnosis. This finding reinforced that 30-day readmission is a complex outcome that may involve multiple clinical pathways.
Finally, I focused on reproducibility and communication. I created a consolidated protocol for the M0, M1, and M2 analyses; generated automated quality-assurance outputs, feature specifications, and audit files; produced a reproducible methods document and technical report; created visual guides explaining the encounter-level joins; and prepared closing presentation materials for DREAM and the VIDA research group.
Overall, Week 8 marked the transition from separate exploratory analyses to an integrated research workflow. I now have a patient-separated, leakage-conscious, and reproducible pipeline that connects prior utilization, EHR diagnosis information, and SDOH data. This week also strengthened my understanding of how careful cohort design, validation, documentation, and interpretation are just as important as the selection of a machine learning algorithm.
