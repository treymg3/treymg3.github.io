Week 6: Expanding SDOH Domains and Literature-Guided Stratification

During Week 6, I expanded my DREAM research workflow beyond the earlier Alcohol, Depression, and Housing Stability analyses into a broader set of Social Determinants of Health domains. These included food insecurity, financial strain, transportation, utilities, stress, social connection, physical activity, intimate partner violence, and smoking. This was an important step because my project is moving toward a more complete understanding of how different SDOH domains may relate to 30-day combined ED-or-hospital return, and prior healthcare utilization.

A major focus this week was developing consistent stratifications across domains. Instead of grouping all unclear responses together, I separated positive, negative, declined, unable-to-answer, unknown, and missing or not-screened responses. This distinction is important because different types of nonresponse may represent different clinical or documentation patterns. For example, a patient who declines to answer a question may not be the same as a patient who is unable to answer or a patient who was never screened. Preserving these distinctions may help avoid losing meaningful information during preprocessing.

I also compared these response groups across several outcomes and utilization measures. These included 30-day hospital readmission, ED return, combined ED-or-hospital return, and prior healthcare utilization. This allowed me to examine whether certain SDOH categories were associated with different patterns of acute-care use. It also helped me think more carefully about which features may be useful for future machine learning models and which variables may need additional sensitivity analysis.

Another major part of Week 6 involved reviewing foundational and recent literature to understand how SDOH measures, response categories, and risk thresholds have been historically defined and validated. I researched literature related to food insecurity, physical activity, financial hardship, housing instability, alcohol risk, depression severity, prior healthcare utilization, and other SDOH measures. The goal was to avoid making arbitrary grouping decisions and instead support my thresholds using published clinical, survey, or public-health standards when possible.

This literature review helped me understand that some domains have well-established cutoffs, while others require more project-specific decisions. For example, certain screening tools have validated scoring rules or accepted positive flags, while other SDOH questions may not have a single universal threshold. In those cases, I began thinking about sensitivity analyses and transparent documentation so that the reasoning behind each stratification decision is clear.

I also refined my prior-utilization measures by reviewing literature-supported definitions of frequent healthcare use. This helped support the use of high-utilization thresholds such as frequent ED use or frequent hospital use. I also continued working to prevent same-day ED-to-hospital transitions from being counted as two separate acute-care events, since that could artificially inflate prior-utilization counts. This step strengthened the validity of the utilization features that may later be used in modeling.

Overall, Week 6 helped me move from domain-by-domain cleaning into a more systematic SDOH research framework. I am learning that strong data science work requires not only coding skill, but also careful attention to measurement, clinical meaning, literature support, and transparent documentation. These steps are helping me build a more defensible and reproducible foundation for the future machine learning phase of the project.
