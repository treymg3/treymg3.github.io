Week 9: Strengthening Model Validation, Diagnosis-Transition Analysis, and Research Communication

During Week 9, I focused on strengthening the validation, interpretation, and communication of my 30-day hospital readmission research. After building the complete modeling pipeline during Week 8, I used this week to examine the results more deeply, evaluate uncertainty, analyze clinical operating thresholds, expand the secondary diagnosis analysis, and refine my final technical presentation.

A major part of this week involved expanding the analysis of what happens when a patient returns to the hospital. Among 11,973 readmitted index encounters, I compared the principal diagnosis from the original hospitalization with the diagnosis recorded during the next qualifying admission. I classified each relationship as an exact ICD-10 match, the same CCSR category, the same clinical body system, a different body system, or unknown.

This analysis showed that most readmissions did not represent an exact recurrence of the original diagnosis. Exact ICD-10 matches accounted for 17.1% of readmissions, while 11.0% were in the same CCSR category and 25.7% remained within the same body system. The largest group, representing 42.8% of readmissions, involved a different body system. This finding reinforced that hospital readmission is a complex outcome and may involve a new or clinically different problem rather than a simple continuation of the original diagnosis.

I then developed a separate, leakage-safe machine learning task to determine whether index-available information could help distinguish different-body-system readmissions from more clinically related returns among patients who were already known to have been readmitted. This was not a model of whether a patient would be readmitted. Instead, it predicted the type of return among readmitted encounters.

I evaluated logistic regression, Random Forest, histogram gradient boosting, and XGBoost for this secondary task. The models produced held-out AUROCs of approximately 0.808–0.810. The narrow range across the four methods suggested that the available predictors contained a meaningful signal, while the specific choice of classifier contributed relatively little additional improvement. Importantly, the readmission diagnosis itself was used only to define the secondary outcome and was never included as a predictor.

Another focus this week was strengthening the uncertainty analysis. Because some patients had more than one qualifying encounter, I used patient-level bootstrap sampling when calculating confidence intervals. This approach preserves the grouping of encounters within patients instead of incorrectly treating every encounter as fully independent. For the primary readmission models, the full XGBoost model achieved a held-out AUROC of 0.666, with an average precision of 0.246. However, the confidence intervals and similar results across classifier families showed that the algorithm differences were relatively small once the same feature set was used.

I also evaluated several clinical operating thresholds. A probability cutoff of 0.50 was extremely specific but detected very few readmissions. Lower thresholds increased sensitivity but also produced more false-positive alerts. To avoid using the test data to make decisions, I selected the Youden, 80%-sensitivity, and 90%-sensitivity thresholds using validation data before applying them to the held-out test set.

This analysis helped me understand that a model cannot be evaluated using AUROC alone. A healthcare organization would need to select an operating threshold based on the intended intervention, available resources, consequences of missed readmissions, and burden created by false-positive alerts. Even the strongest model in this project should therefore be viewed as a research model for risk stratification rather than a tool ready for autonomous clinical use.

Finally, I refined the project’s technical presentation and documentation. I added clearer information about the source data, record linkage, cohort construction, feature definitions, preprocessing decisions, patient-level data separation, leakage controls, literature comparisons, limitations, and future validation requirements. I also improved the consistency and readability of the presentation and its bibliography.

Overall, Week 9 helped me move from building a machine learning pipeline to critically evaluating and communicating it. I learned that trustworthy healthcare machine learning requires more than selecting the best-performing algorithm. It also requires transparent cohort construction, careful leakage prevention, patient-safe validation, uncertainty estimates, clinically meaningful threshold decisions, reproducible documentation, and an honest explanation of the model’s limitations.
